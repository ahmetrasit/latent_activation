## The Scaffold's Proper Role

The scaffold is a floor, not a ceiling.

It protects:

- who speaks and who is addressed;
- the direct actions, assertions, requests, and contrasts;
- grammatical roles and contextual propositions;
- the difference between direct meaning and secondary activation.

It is not:

- a local-sense gate;
- a reason to weaken a secondary branch;
- a demand that every latent object appear literally on the surface;
- a replacement for the discoveries already made;
- an instruction to prefer familiar commentary over a role-complete
  coalition.

## Preserve the Brave Calibration

Do not revisit discoveries with the question, "Is this too bold?"

Revisit them with these questions:

1. Which direct proposition does this coalition make newly perceptible?
2. How does the scaffold clarify its participants or direction?
3. Does the secondary image coexist with the direct reading without replacing
   it?
4. Does the passage order make the relation stronger or more specific?
5. Does a later cue change the function of an earlier direct statement?

A discovery should be removed only when the scaffold exposes an actual
contradiction of participant identity, direction, grammar, or proposition.
Ordinary contextual meaning and secondary lexical resonance normally coexist.

## Integration Work

Rewrite the discovery field so that every substantial channel makes clear:

- the direct contextual proposition;
- the secondary facets or coalition;
- the passage-specific trigger;
- the role completion or transformation;
- what becomes newly perceptible;
- the backward reactivation or closure effect.

Preserve concrete microfindings when they create a memorable recognition even
if they are not governing channels.

Keep independent and rival channels separate. Do not merge them into a general
theme merely to make the field easier to summarize.

Do not let grammar, syntax, or the direct scaffold displace the lexical work.
Use them as forces that activate, orient, or transform it.

## Boundaries

State a boundary only when a reader could otherwise mistake a secondary image
for:

- an alternative translation;
- a literal participant identity;
- an asserted historical event;
- or a causal claim not supplied by the passage.

Do not add a boundary merely because the image is remote or vivid. One precise
boundary is enough; repeated caution weakens the synthesis without adding
accuracy.
